Men and women use AI at work at the same rate. That is the wrong number to look at.

The Census Bureau's March 2026 Household Trends and Outlook Pulse Survey is the first public person-level dataset with an AI-at-work module. 6,680 workers, eleven task types, frequency of use, hours saved. I spent the weekend in it. Here is what the gender numbers look like once you stop counting who has tried the tool.

𝟭. 𝗔𝗱𝗼𝗽𝘁𝗶𝗼𝗻 𝗶𝘀 𝗲𝗾𝘂𝗮𝗹
51% of employed women and 53% of employed men have used AI for a work task. The difference is inside the margin of error.

𝟮. 𝗜𝗻𝘁𝗲𝗻𝘀𝗶𝘁𝘆 𝗶𝘀 𝗻𝗼𝘁
🔹 Daily use: 9% of women, 16% of men.
🔹 Among people who use AI at all, 17% of women use it every day. 30% of men do.
🔹 Men use it for 4.7 task types on average. Women, 3.9.

𝟯. 𝗧𝗵𝗲 𝗴𝗮𝗽 𝗱𝗼𝗲𝘀 𝗻𝗼𝘁 𝗴𝗼 𝗮𝘄𝗮𝘆
I controlled for twenty things. Age, race, education, income, occupation, sector, telework days, marital status, children by age, household size, health, home language, mental health, life satisfaction. The daily-use gap went from 7.2 points to 7.5. An Oaxaca-Blinder decomposition puts essentially all of it in the unexplained column.

It is not job sorting. The gap is largest inside computer occupations, where 32% of women and 56% of men use AI daily. It is not parenthood either. Parents of children under five show a smaller gap than workers with no children at home.

𝟰. 𝗧𝗵𝗲 𝗴𝗮𝗽 𝗶𝘀 𝗶𝗻 𝘄𝗵𝗮𝘁 𝗺𝗲𝗻 𝗮𝗱𝗱
On the tasks that dominate AI use, women and men who use AI report them at the same rates. Writing communications, 61% vs 64%. Generating ideas, 61% vs 63%. Admin work, 51% vs 54%.

The male surplus sits in four tasks.
🔹 Searching for technical help: 62% vs 80%
🔹 Coding or modeling: 12% vs 30%
🔹 Data analysis: 31% vs 48%
🔹 Training yourself: 24% vs 37%
All four hold after controlling for occupation and education.

𝟱. 𝗪𝗵𝘆 𝗶𝘁 𝗺𝗮𝘁𝘁𝗲𝗿𝘀
Time saved follows frequency. Among last-week users, men report 2.2 hours saved a week and women 1.5, and most of that difference is simply that men use it more days. If your AI rollout dashboard counts who has tried the tool, it will show parity. The productivity case rests on daily, broad, self-directed use, and that is where the gap lives.

Count who used it yesterday, not who has used it.

Caveats, because they matter: one cross-section, self-reported hours, and Census has flagged a pending correction to this wave's weights. I ran everything weighted and unweighted. The intensity gap does not move.
